The Instinct to Pry

There are two ways to encounter a broken or opaque object: you can accept the sealed enclosure, or you can pick up a heat gun, find the seam, and pull the adhesive apart.

The first time you open a modern smartphone to replace a degraded battery, everything in the industrial design tells you to turn back. Tiny pentalobe screws, hidden ribbon cables thin as tracing paper, and chemical pull-tabs that snap under tension. The device was engineered to be bought, depleted, and replaced.

Prying it open anyway is not just about saving hardware from a landfill; it is an act of cognitive demystification. Once you hold the lithium-ion pouch in your hand and trace the flex circuits to the board, the magic evaporates. In its place sits something far more interesting: a set of deliberate trade-offs made by engineers who were balancing thermal dissipation, space constraints, and manufacturing speed.

Audio Signal Chains and Logic Pro

I had the exact same impulse entering music production with Logic Pro.

Opening a modern Digital Audio Workstation without a roadmap feels like staring at an airplane cockpit. You can click around on default synthesizer presets, or you can dismantle the routing:

  • Why does sidechain compression allow the bass to breathe beneath the kick drum?
  • How does a stereo bus send preserve phase coherence compared to inserting four individual reverb plugins?
  • What happens when you route an auxiliary channel back into itself until the signal distorts?

Music production is system architecture wrapped in frequency. The moment you grasp the signal chain — from the raw oscillator to the envelope generator, through the filter cutoff, and into the mixer channel strip — software stops being a wall of arbitrary dials. It becomes an interactive circuit board.

The Link to Enterprise Software & Product Engineering

When I audited broken Order-to-Cash processes in SAP S/4HANA or resolved stock allocation race conditions in Odoo, the mindset was identical.

When an enterprise user says "the system is slow and nobody uses it", a superficial response is to schedule another training seminar. A disassembly mindset looks at what users are actually doing when nobody is watching:

They have three Excel tabs open because the ERP requires nine clicks to check inventory availability. They created a manual workaround because the official interface works against human short-term memory.

You cannot fix a user workflow without opening the enclosure, looking at the raw state transitions, and understanding which historical compromise created the friction.

Why I Build Today

This is why I build applications like the Span Suite.

I don't build software just to add more items to an already noisy digital world. I build because I want to take apart the daily friction of planning, focusing, and working — stripping away the decorative chrome until only the tactile, honest core remains.
