Available-to-Promise: The Cost of Blind Commitments

Why promising delivery dates before checking actual warehouse stock creates quiet chaos across sales and logistics.

The Illusion of "In Stock"

In enterprise software and e-commerce architectures, "In Stock" is a dangerous simplification.

If your database displays 100 units of an item in the warehouse, but 60 are physically committed to an outbound shipment scheduled for tomorrow and 45 are reserved in draft shopping carts, your actual available stock is not 100. It is minus five.

Confirming a sales order based on unreserved physical stock is like writing a check without checking your account balance. You are betting that fresh production will arrive before the truck departs. When that bet fails, deliveries stall and warehouse chaos follows.

The Domino Effect: Emergency split shipments, expensive rush deliveries, and endless blame games between sales reps and warehouse operators. All because two systems weren't talking in real time.

The Three Pillars of Robust ATP

To build a commitment engine that holds under peak load, the system must separate physical reality from temporal projection:

  • Physical On-Hand Stock: What is sitting on the warehouse pallet at this exact second.
  • Committed Demand: Units promised to confirmed sales orders, active picking waves, or manufacturing work orders.
  • Confirmed Inbound Supply: Purchase orders or production runs with validated receipt windows and supplier reliability factors.
$$\text{ATP} = \text{Physical Stock} + \text{Planned Inbound} - \text{Committed Outbound}$$

Why This Matters for Software Interfaces

This is not just an ERP problem; it is a universal software pattern.

Whether reserving seats on a high-speed train, scheduling focus blocks on a calendar in the Span Suite, or allocating warehouse inventory in Odoo, the principle is identical: never expose an affirmative state to the user without reserving the underlying capacity.
