1. The Field Problem: When Track Numbers Lie to Passengers

During a tight 4-minute connection at a major hub (like Brussels-Midi or Liège-Guillemins), commuters stand in crowded vestibules with bags, bracing for the train to halt. The official app displays:

The Current Interface: « Arrivée Voie 3 · Départ Voie 4 »

The user's mental model under stress: "Voie 3 and Voie 4 are different numbers. I must rush down the crowded stairs, check the big departures board in the underground corridor, and run up another platform."

The physical reality: In European rail architecture, Voie 3 and Voie 4 often share the exact same island platform. The passenger ran down a flight of stairs in a panic with heavy luggage, only to find out their connection was literally right behind them, 10 meters away on the same concrete slab.

Core Architectural Insight: A train station has two distinct layers:
• The Rail Layer (Track / Voie): Technical ID of the steel rails used by dispatchers.
• The Passenger Layer (Platform / Quai): The physical space where human feet walk.
The current app exposes technical track numbers without translating them into physical spatial guidance.

4. Scene 2: On the Platform — Should I Take the Stairs or Stay Put?

Stepping off an arriving train, the immediate commuter reflex when seeing a different track number (e.g. arrival on Track 3, connection on Track 4) is to sprint toward the stairs and down into the underground tunnel.

Yet in almost all Belgian and European stations, Tracks 3 and 4 share the exact same physical concrete slab (an island platform). 5. Ergonomic Principles & Edge-Case Handling

  • Exit Direction Relative to Travel: Stating « Doors on LEFT in direction of travel » allows passengers with heavy luggage to line up at the correct door 3 minutes ahead, avoiding last-second scrambles through crowded vestibules.
  • Platform Length Edge-Cases (e.g. Gare d'Ans): In certain historical stations, platforms are physically shorter than long-distance IC trainsets. The interface warns passengers prior to arrival if their coach will overhang the platform, directing them to move forward to front cars (Cars 1–4) before the train stops.
  • Fail-Safe Feasibility Window: If an inbound delay reduces transfer time below the calculated walking distance between platforms, the app does not let passengers sprint blindly toward closed doors; it proactively highlights the verified fallback train.

7. Key Takeaways: What This Solves in the Real World

Bridging Data and Human Physical Reality: Rail APIs expose technical dispatcher data (track numbers, train composition). But commuters don't walk on track numbers—they walk on physical slabs of concrete with bags, strollers, and tight connection windows. Translating raw data into immediate spatial guidance ("Same physical platform: do not take the stairs") transforms a high-stress bottleneck into a calm, effortless transfer.
