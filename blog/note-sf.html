When the Consultant Is Also the User

On what it means to have been on the wrong side of the tools you implement — and why that changes how you work.

I stopped filling in a job application mid-way through because of SuccessFactors. Not because I changed my mind about the role. Because I'd been re-entering my entire work history from scratch, field by field, with no indication of how far I still had to go — and at some point the effort tipped past the motivation. I closed the tab.

I am an SAP consultant. SuccessFactors is an SAP product. The irony wasn't lost on me.

What stayed with me wasn't frustration — it was recognition. Because I had seen that exact moment before, from the other side. On an implementation I was part of, a B2B sales team had been managing their orders entirely through email and spreadsheets. When we moved them into SAP, the pushback was immediate: too rigid, too many steps, not how we work. The system was sound. The resistance was real.

At the time I understood their position intellectually. What the SuccessFactors moment gave me was something more specific: I knew exactly what it felt like to be in front of a system you didn't choose, that doesn't fit your workflow, that asks more of you than it gives back — at least at first.

That experience doesn't make me a better technician. It makes me a more useful one in the room when a team is resisting a system that is, objectively, better for them. Because I'm not working from a theory of resistance — I'm working from the memory of wanting to close the tab.

The practical takeaway, for anyone doing this kind of work: the quality of an implementation isn't just measured in go-live. It's measured in whether people use it six months later — and that depends almost entirely on whether they ever felt heard before it was switched on.

I walked the B2B team through their first orders in SAP myself. Not because it was in my scope, but because it was the only way to make the system feel less foreign. It worked. Not immediately — but order by order.
